**Voice rules applied to every script in this file:**

- Zero em dashes. Commas, colons and parentheses instead. This is both the brand voice rule in `.agents/product-marketing.md` and the primary AI tell.
- No hype vocabulary, no exclamation points, US spelling, first person.
- Never "my software", "my platform", "my product", "the board", "the dashboard". There is no board in this build.
- **Resident, not tenant.** The most visible tell of someone writing from outside the industry.
- Every number traceable to `customer-language.md`.
- No client results. Nothing here may read as a customer outcome.
- The fair-housing material is never legal advice, and post 6 says so on the page.

This matches the deviation recorded for build 3 post 1.
- Posts 3 and 6 keep **Problem-Proof**, with slide 1 carrying a specific claim rather than a business result, and the closing receipt being fixture output labeled as fixture output.
- The plan called post 13 a "comparison," which is not one of the five frameworks. It uses **Value-Stack with a contrarian cover**, the same structure build 3 used for its competitive carousel.

## Post 1. Your renewal rate is a number nobody in your office can explain

**Format:** document carousel, 9 slides. **Framework:** Hack List, adapted. **Pillar:** the renewal conversation. **CTA:** none.

**Document title (50-char limit):** `Why residents leave, and why you disagree` (40 characters)

**On the framework.** Problem-Proof needs a personal result on slide 1 and a receipt on the last one. There is neither. Hack List fits a diagnostic honestly: a contrarian opening, then named items, each of which re-earns the swipe. The items here are mismatches between two lists rather than techniques.

### The post copy that sits above the document

> Ask a property manager why residents leave and you get a list. New job. Moved in with a partner. Bought a house. Things nobody could have changed.
>
> Ask the residents who left and you get a different list. Rent. Repairs that never got done. The feeling that nobody was listening.
>
> Both lists describe the same people, walking out of the same buildings, in the same year. One of them is made of things you could have acted on. That is the one the residents wrote.
>
> This is not a story about property managers being wrong. It is a story about where the information sits. The manager's list is built from what residents said on the way out, in a conversation nobody enjoys, at a point where being honest has no upside. The resident's list is what they would have said three months earlier, if anyone had asked in a way that made it easy.
>
> Retention across the industry has slipped from 60% to 57%. That sounds small until you price it. A non-renewal runs somewhere near four thousand dollars once you count the make-ready, the empty weeks, the advertising and the leasing cost.
>
> So the question I would want answered, before buying any software at all, is whether my own explanation of my renewal rate would survive contact with the people who left.
>
> Nine slides. Three specific mismatches, and a test you can run this week without spending anything.

*Word count: 258. Characters: 1462. Em dashes: 0. First 210 characters end inside "Things nobody could have changed," so the first list lands complete and the contradiction is held back past the fold.*

### Slides

**Slide 1 — Cover**
> Ask your team why residents leave.
> Then ask the residents.
> The two lists barely overlap.

**Slide 2 — The reframe**
> Both lists describe the same departures.
> One is full of things you could not control.
> The other is full of things you could.

**Slide 3 — Mismatch 1**
> They say: life circumstances.
> Residents say: rent.
> One of those you get to talk about early.

**Slide 4 — Mismatch 2**
> They say: nothing we could do.
> Residents say: maintenance.
> Repairs that were reported and then went quiet.

**Slide 5 — Mismatch 3**
> They say: they just moved on.
> Residents say: nobody listened.
> That one is free to fix and nobody bills for it.

**Slide 6 — Why the gap survives**
> Exit surveys ask after they have gone.
> Satisfaction surveys ask on a date unrelated to any lease.
> Neither asks the month the decision is being made.

**Slide 7 — What it costs**
> Retention: 60% to 57%.
> About 43% of units turn every year.
> Roughly $4,000 for each one that does.

**Slide 8 — The test**
> Call the last five residents who left.
> Ask what would have changed their mind.
> Compare it with what your team assumed.

**Slide 9 — Close**
> If those two lists differ, that gap is your renewal rate.
> No software required to run this test.

### Discipline check

- Retention figures from Zego's 2026 report. The per-non-renewal cost from NAA. Both in `customer-language.md`.
- The "no field for this" observation is one line on slide 6 and is not developed. Build 3 post 1 owns that argument in full.
- No product named in nine slides or in the caption. Nothing is sold.
- Slide 9 gives away the whole method for free. That is deliberate. A reader who runs the test and never contacts me has still been told the truth.
- No daily rent arithmetic anywhere. Build 3 post 4 owns that unit.

### Design notes

Cover: two short columns side by side with almost no overlap between them, visible at thumbnail size. Interior slides share one template with the "they say / residents say" split in a fixed position so the structure reads at a glance. No stock photography of anyone shaking hands.

## Post 3. "We're happy here, but…"

**Format:** document carousel, 8 slides. **Framework:** Problem-Proof, adapted. **Pillar:** reading the reply. **CTA:** none.

**Document title (50-char limit):** `The complaint that opens with a compliment` (41 characters)

**On the framework.** Slide 1 carries a claim about a specific message rather than a business result, because there is no business result to report. The closing receipt is real output from the build running on synthetic residents, and the slide says so.

### The post copy that sits above the document

> Here is a sentence a resident sent back after a check-in:
>
> "We're happy here but the extractor fan in the bathroom has been broken since summer and I've reported it twice."
>
> Read the first four words and you file it under good news. Most people scanning forty replies on a Tuesday would, and so would most sentiment scoring, which reads the opening and the overall shape and lands on positive.
>
> Now read the back half. Two things are sitting in it, and both are worse than a broken fan.
>
> The first is duration. Since summer. Whatever this is, it has been part of living there for months, which means it is no longer a repair. It is a fact about the apartment.
>
> The second is the part that costs you the renewal. Reported it twice. They already did the thing you want residents to do. They used the process. The process did nothing, and they are telling you that as politely as it is possible to tell someone.
>
> A resident who complains loudly is engaged. A resident who says "we're happy here, but" and then describes something they gave up on months ago has already started leaving. They are just being nice about it.
>
> Eight slides on how that sentence gets classified, and the two words I would search for in your own inbox tonight.

*Word count: 251. Characters: 1418. Em dashes: 0. First 210 characters end inside the quoted reply itself, so the sentence lands and the reading of it is withheld past the fold.*

### Slides

**Slide 1 — Cover**
> "We're happy here but the extractor fan has been broken since summer and I've reported it twice."
> That is not a positive reply.

**Slide 2 — The problem**
> The first four words do all the work.
> Sentiment scoring reads it as positive.
> So does a person scanning forty of them.

**Slide 3 — Detail one**
> "Since summer."
> This stopped being a repair a while ago.
> It is now a feature of the apartment.

**Slide 4 — Detail two**
> "Reported it twice."
> They used your process.
> Your process did nothing, and they noticed.

**Slide 5 — What it actually is**
> A resident who shouts is engaged.
> A resident who is polite about something they gave up on
> has already started leaving.

**Slide 6 — The classification**
> Grievance. Maintenance. High urgency. High confidence.
> Next action: check the work order history, call today.
> Synthetic resident, real output.

**Slide 7 — What a survey would have caught**
> The same resident, on a survey, months from their lease end:
> a fine score.
> Nothing to act on, and no reason to act now.

**Slide 8 — Close**
> Search your resident replies for "still" and "again."
> Read every one of those twice.

### Discipline check

- The reply is the fixture used in the build. Slide 6 labels the output as synthetic, so nothing reads as a customer incident.
- The claim is narrow and checkable: a rules-plus-model pass classified a positive-opening message as a high-urgency grievance and produced a specific next action. **No general "AI understands context" claim anywhere.** That would be both unprovable and dull.
- Slide 7 does not attack surveys. It states what a differently-timed instrument captures. Surveys are complementary and `customer-language.md` says so.
- Slide 8 is the free takeaway and works with no software at all.

### Design notes

Cover: the full quoted sentence, with the first four words in the same weight as the rest. Do not highlight or color the second half on slide 1. The reader has to do the reading themselves for the turn to land. Highlighting may begin on slide 3, where the specific phrases are being pulled out.

## Post 6. The safest-looking version of this had a legal problem

**Format:** document carousel, 10 slides. **Framework:** Problem-Proof, told as a reversal. **Pillar:** the legal surface. **CTA:** none.

**Document title (50-char limit):** `The cautious design was the risky one` (36 characters)

**This is the strongest post in the plan and the easiest to get wrong.** Every claim on the page traces to something published. Nothing on it tells a reader what the law requires of them.

### The post copy that sits above the document

> When I built the reply handling for a renewal check-in, I put in what felt like the careful rule.
>
> If a resident's reply looks like a fair housing matter, an accommodation request, a habitability complaint, anything with legal weight, the system drafts nothing. No suggested reply. It flags it and hands it to a person.
>
> That felt right. Keep the model away from the regulated conversation.
>
> Then someone testing it asked what happens when you press the button on one of those. And the answer was: a resident complaining about a broken fan gets a drafted reply in about two seconds, and a resident asking for a grab bar for their mother gets nothing, and waits for a human to get to it.
>
> Two residents. Two speeds. The difference between them being that one of them mentioned a disability.
>
> I went looking, and that pattern has a name. Compliance analysts writing about AI in housing describe exactly this, frictionless handling for ordinary requests and a slower path for protected class requests, as something that can constitute disparate impact. HUD said in 2024 that algorithmic tools fall under the Fair Housing Act, that disparate outcomes are actionable whether or not you meant them, and that not knowing is not a defense.
>
> So the careful-looking design was the one with the exposure, and it took a question from someone who had never seen the code to find it.
>
> Ten slides on what changed. This is not legal advice and I am not the person to take it from.

*Word count: 268. Characters: 1533. Em dashes: 0. First 210 characters end inside "If a resident's reply looks like a fair housing matter," so the setup lands and the reversal is held past the fold.*

### Slides

**Slide 1 — Cover**
> I built it to refuse.
> Refusing was the risk.

**Slide 2 — The rule that felt right**
> If a reply looks like a fair housing matter,
> draft nothing.
> Flag it, hand it to a person.

**Slide 3 — What that looked like in practice**
> Broken fan: drafted reply in two seconds.
> Grab bar for a resident's mother: nothing.
> Wait for a human.

**Slide 4 — The thing I had built**
> Two residents. Two speeds.
> The difference being that one of them
> mentioned a disability.

**Slide 5 — It has a name**
> Frictionless service for ordinary requests,
> a slower queue for protected class requests.
> Analysts describe that as possible disparate impact.

**Slide 6 — What HUD said in 2024**
> Algorithmic tools fall under the Fair Housing Act.
> Disparate outcomes are actionable regardless of intent.
> Not knowing is not a defense.

**Slide 7 — Why the model cannot be the detector**
> A language model cannot reliably tell
> a pet rent question from an assistance animal request.
> So detection runs on fixed rules, before the model.

**Slide 8 — What changed**
> Every category drafts at the same speed.
> The flag changes what the manager sees.
> It does not change what the resident waits for.

**Slide 9 — The general version**
> Audit where your automation does less,
> not only where it does more.
> Refusing is a behavior. It is not automatically the safe one.

**Slide 10 — Close**
> Found by someone asking what a button did.
> Not by a test suite, and not by me.
> Not legal advice. Ask your own counsel.

### Discipline check

- **Not legal advice, stated twice**, in the caption and on slide 10. No claim anywhere that the current design makes anyone compliant.
- HUD's 2024 position and the disparate-impact analysis are reported as published, with no invented case law, no named firm, and no enforcement history that was not found.
- No moralizing about the industry. The story is that my own first instinct was wrong, and the reader is a peer rather than a defendant.
- Slide 10 credits the person who asked the question. That is what happened and it is more useful than implying a process caught it.
- The specific reply on slide 3 is the fixture wording from the build, not a real resident.

### Design notes

Cover: two lines, enormous, nothing else on the slide. This is the strongest cover in the set and should carry no decoration at all. Slides 3 and 4 are the emotional center: consider a simple two-column layout with the two residents side by side and the timing under each. Slide 6 should look like a citation, plain and unstyled, because the credibility comes from it being a quote rather than a design element.

**Hashtags:** #propertymanagement #fairhousing #multifamily #proptech

## Post 13. The category is building something that talks to your residents. This one is not allowed to.

**Format:** document carousel, 9 slides. **Framework:** Value-Stack with a contrarian cover. **Pillar:** proof and market. **CTA:** soft.

**Document title (50-char limit):** `Which renewal tool is actually for you` (38 characters)

**This post has to be genuinely useful to someone who never hires me.** If a reader closes it and buys EliseAI, the post worked.

### The post copy that sits above the document

> The best funded products in this space will hold a conversation with your residents about renewing. That is the whole point of them, and they are good at it.
>
> EliseAI has a renewals product that checks in proactively, answers questions about lease terms and walks a resident through renewing without anyone on your team chasing. Colleen AI markets engaging residents ahead of the standard ninety day timeline. Both work. Neither needs me to explain that renewals matter.
>
> What I built does something narrower and, in one specific way, opposite. It is forbidden from mentioning the renewal at all. Rent, lease terms, deadlines, money: all blocked, at drafting and again on the way out. It asks one open question ninety days out and routes whatever comes back to a person.
>
> That is not a smaller version of a renewal assistant. It is a different job that happens to run on the same calendar. One is built to close a renewal. The other is built to find out what is broken while there is still time to fix it.
>
> So this is a map rather than a pitch. If you want more renewals closed by software that talks to residents, buy the renewal assistant, and I would rather tell you that now than three calls in.
>
> One more thing worth checking before you buy anything. Renewal workflow is usually already sitting inside your property management system as a one or two dollar per unit add-on. If offers going out on time is the actual problem, start there.
>
> Nine slides, including the ones that end with "buy theirs."

*Word count: 271. Characters: 1531. Em dashes: 0. First 210 characters end inside "That is the whole point of them," so the concession to competitors lands before anything about my own build.*

### Discipline check

- **Build 2 post 3 owns "the category removed the human and I did not."** This carousel does not re-argue human oversight anywhere. The argument here is purpose: those products close renewals, this one is forbidden from discussing one.
- No competitor is disparaged. Slide 6 tells qualifying readers to buy them, and slide 3 describes what they do in their own terms.
- Every product claim traces to `customer-language.md`, sourced from the vendors' own pages.
- The pricing contrast was cut from the slides. The $4 to $12 per unit stack figure sits in `customer-language.md` and would turn a buyer's guide into a price argument. If it is ever used, it belongs in a text post, not here.
- No competitor logos. Using them implies a relationship that does not exist.

Slides 6, 7 and 8 carry a consistent "who this is for" band at the top so the guide structure is visible at a glance, matching the pattern used in build 3's competitive carousel.
