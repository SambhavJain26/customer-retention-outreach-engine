## Post 1. Your renewal rate is a number nobody in your office can explain

**Format:** document carousel, 9 slides. **Framework:** Hack List, adapted. **Pillar:** the renewal conversation. **CTA:** none.

**Document title (50-char limit):** `Why residents leave, and why you disagree` (40 characters)

**On the framework.** Problem-Proof needs a personal result on slide 1 and a receipt on the last one. There is neither. Hack List fits a diagnostic honestly: a contrarian opening, then named items, each of which re-earns the swipe. The items here are mismatches between two lists rather than techniques.

### The post copy that sits above the document

> Ask a property manager why residents leave and you get a list. New job. Moved in with a partner. Bought a house. Things nobody could have changed.
>
> Ask the residents who left and you get a different list. Rent. Repairs that never got done. The feeling that nobody was listening.
>
> Both lists describe the same people, walking out of the same buildings, in the same year. One of them is made of things you could have acted on. That is the one the residents wrote.
>
> This is not a story about property managers being wrong. It is a story about where the information sits. The manager's list is built from what residents said on the way out, in a conversation nobody enjoys, at a point where being honest has no upside. The resident's list is what they would have said three months earlier, if anyone had asked in a way that made it easy.
>
> Retention across the industry has slipped from 60% to 57%. That sounds small until you price it. A non-renewal runs somewhere near four thousand dollars once you count the make-ready, the empty weeks, the advertising and the leasing cost.
>
> So the question I would want answered, before buying any software at all, is whether my own explanation of my renewal rate would survive contact with the people who left.
>
> Nine slides. Three specific mismatches, and a test you can run this week without spending anything.

## Post 6. The safest-looking version of this had a legal problem

**Format:** document carousel, 10 slides. **Framework:** Problem-Proof, told as a reversal. **Pillar:** the legal surface. **CTA:** none.

**Document title (50-char limit):** `The cautious design was the risky one` (36 characters)

**This is the strongest post in the plan and the easiest to get wrong.** Every claim on the page traces to something published. Nothing on it tells a reader what the law requires of them.

### The post copy that sits above the document

> When I built the reply handling for a renewal check-in, I put in what felt like the careful rule.
>
> If a resident's reply looks like a fair housing matter, an accommodation request, a habitability complaint, anything with legal weight, the system drafts nothing. No suggested reply. It flags it and hands it to a person.
>
> That felt right. Keep the model away from the regulated conversation.
>
> Then someone testing it asked what happens when you press the button on one of those. And the answer was: a resident complaining about a broken fan gets a drafted reply in about two seconds, and a resident asking for a grab bar for their mother gets nothing, and waits for a human to get to it.
>
> Two residents. Two speeds. The difference between them being that one of them mentioned a disability.
>
> I went looking, and that pattern has a name. Compliance analysts writing about AI in housing describe exactly this, frictionless handling for ordinary requests and a slower path for protected class requests, as something that can constitute disparate impact. HUD said in 2024 that algorithmic tools fall under the Fair Housing Act, that disparate outcomes are actionable whether or not you meant them, and that not knowing is not a defense.
>
> So the careful-looking design was the one with the exposure, and it took a question from someone who had never seen the code to find it.
>
> Ten slides on what changed. This is not legal advice and I am not the person to take it from.

*Word count: 268. Characters: 1533. Em dashes: 0. First 210 characters end inside "If a resident's reply looks like a fair housing matter," so the setup lands and the reversal is held past the fold.*

## One mailbox, and saying so

Every email in this recording moves through a single Gmail account. The check-in goes out from it, the resident reply comes back into it, the assisted reply goes out from it again. There is no second person and no second address.

That is fine, and it is only fine if it is said. **One disclosure line in the LinkedIn text and one in the YouTube description, both mandatory:** one mailbox plays both sides, and in a real install the resident is on their own address with nothing else changing.

It also settles what the copy may claim about matching. Replies are matched by the reference code carried in the subject line, not by who sent them and not by whether a message is unread. That is the answer if anyone asks how a single mailbox talks to itself without confusing the system. Never write anything suggesting a resident is recognized by their email address alone.
