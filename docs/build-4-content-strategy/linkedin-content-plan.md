# LinkedIn Content Plan — Pre-Renewal Resident Retention

Created: 2026-08-22

Fifteen posts: one demo video, fourteen supporting. Each brief carries the elements a script needs, so turning one into a draft is a writing job, not a thinking job. Finished copy will live in `linkedin-post-scripts.md`.

This is the fourth of the planned builds. Builds 1 (vendor insurance paperwork), 2 (maintenance triage) and 3 (make-ready sequencing) are planned in their own folders and all have finished scripts. **Some posts from those builds are already published.** Publishing order for this build is the operator's decision and is not planned here. What this plan does control is that nothing in it restates ground the earlier three already covered — see "Relationship to builds 1, 2 and 3."

## What is being sold

**Custom AI automation, built and implemented inside a client's existing business.** Not software. Not a SaaS product. Nothing to log into, no seats, no subscription.

This governs every post. The renewal system is a **worked example of how I approach a problem** — evidence of judgment, not a product being launched. Language never implies "my tool" or "my platform." It says: this is the kind of thing I build, inside your Airtable, your inbox, your Telegram, and you own it when it's done.

**The buyer:** US residential property management companies, roughly 150-600 doors, with resident email addresses and lease end dates in exportable form. Owner or principal signs; the property manager or ops lead is the daily user and internal champion.

## What this build owns that the others cannot

Builds 1, 2 and 3 are all about **operational objects**. A certificate that arrived. A work order to classify. A unit moving through stages. Every one has a defined completion state, and the counterparty on the other end is a vendor.

This build is about **a person deciding whether to stay**, and the counterparty is a resident. Three consequences, all of them new material:

1. **The decision has no timestamp.** A vendor either finished or did not. A resident's renewal decision forms quietly over months and surfaces once, as a signature or a notice. Nothing in the earlier three builds is this soft, and the copy has to be honest about that rather than pretending to a precision it does not have.

2. **The message that looks positive is the problem.** A vendor saying "done" is unambiguous. A resident saying "we're happy here, but…" is a complaint wearing a compliment. Build 2 classifies severity of things that arrive *as* complaints. This build's central case is a message that does not present as one.

3. **There is a regulated class of reply.** Accommodation requests, habitability, discrimination concerns, legal threats. No earlier build has a counterparty with protected-class status, so none of them could make a fair-housing argument. This one has to, carefully.

## 1. Your renewal rate is a number nobody in your office can explain

**Format:** carousel, 9 slides · **Framework:** Problem-Proof · **Pillar:** the renewal conversation · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** Open on two lists of reasons for the same set of departures. One list is what property managers say. The other is what the residents who left say. They barely overlap.

**The turn:** Managers attribute non-renewals to things outside their control — a job move, a partner, buying a house. Residents who left report rent, maintenance and communication. Both groups are describing the same people walking out of the same buildings. Which means the manager's own explanation of their renewal rate is not a description of the problem. It is the problem, showing up as a confident answer that happens to be unfalsifiable.

**Evidence:** Zego's 2026 resident experience research: average retention down from 60% to 57%, with managers citing life changes and renters citing rent, poor maintenance and security. NAA putting a single non-renewal at roughly $3,872-4,000 all in. The structural point that exit surveys record reasons *after* the person has gone, satisfaction surveys record a score unconnected to any lease clock, and renewal reports record outcomes — so there is no existing place where "a fixable problem, found in time" would be written down.

**Takeaway:** Before buying anything, ask the last five residents who left what would have changed their mind, and compare it with what your team assumed. If those two lists differ, the gap is your renewal rate.

**Legibility:** Zero domain knowledge required. Anyone who has rented follows it.

**Discipline:** The "no field for this" observation is **one line**, not a section. Build 3 post 1 owns that argument in full and this must not restate it. Do not claim the automation closes the gap; the post is about the gap existing.

**Why this is the anchor post:** It indicts nobody, every reader can run the test on their own portfolio, and the result is uncomfortable in a way that has nothing to do with buying anything.

**Status: recorded on 2026-08-29.** This brief has been rewritten to describe the video that exists. The original beat list is superseded, and the differences are itemized in the "What changed against the original script" table in `linkedin-post-scripts.md`. Nothing here asks for a reshoot.

**Hook angle:** Open on the Airtable lease export and the four points where the system asks a question. No preamble, no introduction, no face.

**The turn:** Every message the viewer sees was written by a model, one of them was rewritten by hand before it went anywhere, and not one of them reached a resident without a person tapping a button first. And the reply that mattered most was the one that read like good news.

**Four surfaces appear:** Airtable, Telegram, Gmail and n8n. "Built inside tools you already have" is a claim, and those frames are the evidence for it. n8n carries more weight than planned, because the recording opens the executions and the OpenAI node rather than panning the workflow list.

**What the recording covers**, in order:

1. **The lease export.** The Leases table, and the four check-in points at a hundred and twenty, ninety, sixty and thirty days.
2. **The draft.** A finished ninety-day message in Telegram with four buttons, none of which fires on its own.
3. **The edit.** `Edit wording`, a sentence trimmed by hand, and a fresh card carrying the edited version.
4. **The send.** `Approve & Send`, then the inbox showing that what arrived is the edited wording rather than the model's.

**Evidence:** Live fixture data on screen. The edited wording surviving into the inbox. The classification landing in Airtable. The log.

All are true of the build. None is on camera.

**Takeaway:** This is real, it runs on ordinary email, and the person who built it volunteers its limits before being asked.

**Framing discipline:** "Here's something I built for a property management company's renewal process." Never "here's my product." Synthetic residents, stated in the copy.

**Two disclosures that were not spoken on camera and therefore must appear in the LinkedIn text and the YouTube description:** the limits list, and the fact that there is no client. ## 3. "We're happy here, but…"

**Format:** carousel, 8 slides · **Framework:** Problem-Proof · **Pillar:** reading the reply · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** Open with the full sentence and nothing else on the slide. Let the reader decide what it means before turning the page.

**The turn:** Sentiment scores this positive. A busy manager scanning forty replies scores it positive too, because the first three words do the work and the rest is read as a minor aside. The complaint is in the back half of a sentence that opens with praise — and it contains the two details that actually predict a departure: the problem has lasted months, and it was reported and nothing happened.

**Evidence:** The real reply from the build, verbatim. Its actual classification: grievance, maintenance, high urgency, high confidence, with the generated instruction to check the work order history and call the resident today. The contrast with what a satisfaction survey would have captured from the same resident: a fine score, on a date unconnected to their lease. The Zego finding that residents report maintenance as a reason for leaving while managers report life changes — the same gap, visible in a single sentence.

**Takeaway:** Do not sort resident replies by how they open. The sentence that starts with a compliment and contains the word "still" or "again" is the one to read twice.

**Legibility:** Zero domain knowledge required.

**Discipline:** This is about **reading meaning, not measuring sentiment**. Do not drift into a general "AI understands context" claim, which is both boring and unprovable. The specific, defensible claim is narrower: a rules-plus-model pass caught a grievance in a message whose opening reads as satisfied, and produced a next action naming the work order history.

## 4. The renewal offer is a question, and it only has one answer

**Format:** text, 250-300 words · **Pillar:** the renewal conversation · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** Open on what the renewal offer actually is when it lands: a number, with a deadline attached, from someone the resident has not heard from in months.

**The turn:** An offer invites exactly one kind of response, which is a response about the offer. Ask someone "here is your new rate, are you staying" and you will hear about the rate, because that is the only thing on the table. Everything that actually drove the decision — the repair, the parking, the noise upstairs — never enters, not because the resident is hiding it, but because nobody asked and the form of the question makes it awkward to volunteer.

**Evidence:** The industry guidance is already to start renewals at ninety days rather than sixty, specifically so there is time to gauge intent and address dissatisfaction. That guidance is standard and this build did not invent it. What differs is what fills the window: the standard 90-day action is the offer itself, and this sends a question that is structurally forbidden from mentioning rent, renewal terms, deadlines or money at all.

**Takeaway:** If the first renewal contact of the year is a price, price is the only feedback that comes back. Whatever you send ninety days out, make sure it cannot be answered with a number.

**Discipline:** **Do not claim the ninety-day window as an insight.** It is published best practice and a reader who runs renewals knows it. The claim is about what the message contains, not when it is sent. Overclaiming the timing is the fastest way to lose this audience.

## 5. What one resident leaving actually costs

**Format:** single image · **Pillar:** the renewal conversation · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** One figure, large, with the components stacked under it. No headline framing above it.

**The turn:** The number is uncomfortable on its own, and worse per percentage point. Three points of retention across a modest portfolio is a used car every year, arriving as nine separate small events that each look like somebody's life circumstances.

**Evidence:** NAA at roughly $3,872-4,000 per non-renewal, covering make-ready, vacancy loss, marketing and leasing costs. Retention at 57%, down from 60%, meaning about 43% of units turn annually. The worked example at ICP scale: a 300-door portfolio losing three retention points is about nine extra non-renewals, roughly $36,000 a year. The larger illustration for contrast: a 5,000-door portfolio making the same slip takes on about 150 extra turns, near $600,000.

**Takeaway:** Work out your own per-turn number once, then multiply it by the retention points you lost last year. That product is the budget for solving it.

**Discipline:** **No daily rent arithmetic in this post.** Build 3 post 4 owns rent-divided-by-30 and per-vacant-day math, and this post must stay in whole non-renewals and retention points. Both figures are arithmetic offered for the reader to redo with their own turnover cost, never benchmarks and never results this automation produced. The $4,000 is an apartment figure; note that scattered-site single-family runs differently.

## 6. The safest-looking version of this had a legal problem

**Format:** carousel, 10 slides · **Framework:** Problem-Proof, told as a reversal · **Pillar:** the legal surface · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** Open on the design decision that felt obviously correct: when a resident's reply looks like a fair-housing matter, the system refuses to draft anything and hands it straight to a human.

**The turn:** That design creates two tiers of service. A resident complaining about a broken fan gets a drafted reply in seconds. A resident asking for a grab bar for their mother gets silence and a longer wait. Published compliance analysis describes exactly that pattern — frictionless handling for ordinary requests, a slower queue for protected-class requests — as capable of constituting disparate impact. The cautious-looking choice was the one with the exposure, and it took someone asking "what happens if I press this" to surface it.

**Evidence:** HUD guidance from May 2024 establishing that algorithmic tools are subject to the Fair Housing Act, that disparate outcomes are actionable regardless of intent, and that not knowing is not a defence. The published warning that language models cannot reliably distinguish a pet-rent question from an assistance-animal accommodation request — which is why detection in this build runs on fixed rules *before* the model is consulted, not on the model noticing. The fix: every category now drafts at the same speed, and the compliance flag changes what the manager sees rather than what the resident experiences.

**Takeaway:** When you add AI to resident communication, audit the paths where it does *less*, not only the paths where it does more. Refusing to help is a behaviour, and it is not automatically the safe one.

**Legibility:** Zero domain knowledge required, but assumes the reader manages housing.

**Discipline:** **This is not legal advice and must say so.** Do not claim the current design makes anyone compliant. Do not name a law firm, invent a case, or imply enforcement history that was not found. Report what HUD published and what compliance analysts wrote, and be explicit that a client's own counsel owns the policy. Do not moralise about the industry — the reversal is a story about my own build being wrong, and the reader is a peer, not a defendant.

**Why this is probably the strongest post across all four builds:** it is a genuine reversal with a legal edge, it teaches something most people adding AI to resident messaging have not considered, and it is entirely uncomplimentary about the author's first instinct.

## 7. The guardrail that checked at the wrong moment

**Format:** native video, 45-60 seconds, cut from the demo · **Pillar:** failure lessons · **CTA:** none

**Rebuilt against the recording.** The refusal this post was planned around was never filmed. The edit made on camera is a safe trim of a sentence. The post stays video on a different clip, because the recording contains the editable field itself: a manager rewriting a draft, and that rewritten wording arriving in a resident's inbox. What is missing is the refusal, not the argument.

**Hook angle:** Open on somebody rewriting an AI's message by hand, then follow their words into a real inbox.

**The turn:** The rules that block rent, renewal terms, deadlines and money ran when the model produced a draft. They did not run again when the message was sent. Between those two moments sits an editable field, and a manager can rewrite the wording in Airtable or straight from the chat. So the system checked the AI's words carefully and then sent whatever a human typed over the top of them, unchecked. The boundary existed; it was just guarding the wrong instant.

**Evidence:** The clip from the demo: `Edit wording`, a draft rewritten by hand, the redrafted card, the approval, and the edited copy appearing in the resident's inbox. That is the gap, on screen. The fix, stated in the post text rather than shown: guardrails now run again at send time against whatever the record currently holds, so edited wording is checked exactly like generated wording.

**Honesty constraint:** the post must say in its own words that the fix is not visible in the clip. No caption, overlay or subtitle may imply a refusal the viewer cannot see.

**Takeaway:** Work out where your automation last checks something, and what a person can change after that point. That gap is the real surface area.

**Discipline:** Build 2 post 9 owns *what* an automation should and should not say to a resident. **This post must not re-argue the contents of the boundary.** Its lesson is entirely about timing: the same check, run at the wrong moment, is not a check. Keep the banned-word list to a passing mention.

**Capture note, resolved:** the unsafe-edit refusal is not in the footage. This post uses demo segments 3 and 4 instead and remains video. If the demo is ever reshot with the refusal in it, that version replaces this cut and is the stronger post.

## 8. The button that appeared to do nothing

**Format:** text, 250-300 words · **Pillar:** failure lessons · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** Open on the question that found it, asked by someone testing the build: what happens if I press Skip on a resident who has already been emailed?

**The turn:** Approve was guarded carefully, because it obviously sends an email. Skip is the do-nothing button, so it had no guard at all — and it quietly reset the resident to the state they were in before contact, wiping the record that anything had been sent. The damage would not have appeared for weeks, at the next generation run, when that resident received a second check-in as though they had never been contacted. The dangerous control was not the one that does something. It was the one that looks like it does nothing.

**Evidence:** The specific state change: status reset, the stored message cleared, the resident returned to the pool. The fix: Skip now refuses on anyone already contacted and says why. The general shape of the mistake, which is that attention follows apparent consequence, and every button that appears harmless gets less of it.

**Takeaway:** In any approval workflow, list the controls that seem to do nothing, then work out what each one silently undoes. That is where the unguarded ones are.

**Discipline:** Do not present this as caught by a clever process. It was caught because someone asked a question before pressing a button. Say that plainly — it is more useful and more honest than implying a test suite found it.

## 9. Who gets contacted is decided by one field

**Format:** single image · **Pillar:** the legal surface · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** A single Airtable column, and a list underneath of everything that is deliberately not consulted.

**The turn:** Cohorts come from the lease end date. Not payment history, not maintenance ticket volume, not tenure, not anything about the person. That constraint costs something real — a smarter system could rank by risk and contact the shakiest residents first. It was refused because the moment resident selection depends on behaviour, the question "why did this resident get contacted and that one not" acquires an answer somebody may have to defend.

**Evidence:** The field, on screen. The list of inputs not used. The plain statement that this is a design constraint accepted for defensibility, not a limitation of what the technology could do.

**Takeaway:** Ask anything that selects which of your residents get contacted what inputs it uses. If the answer includes behaviour, ask who would explain that choice if it were ever questioned.

**Discipline:** Do not imply competitors are doing something unlawful. The claim is about **explainability**, not accusation: a date-based rule is trivially explainable and a behavioural model is not. No legal advice.

## 10. "Stop emailing me" is a permanent answer

**Format:** text, 200-250 words · **Pillar:** reading the reply · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** Open on the shortest reply the system has ever had to handle, and the amount of machinery that sits behind honouring it.

**The turn:** Opting out is easy to implement and easy to implement badly, because the failure is invisible for months. A flag that suppresses the current campaign but is not consulted by the next one looks identical to a working opt-out right up until the resident who asked you to stop gets contacted again — at which point you have converted a mild annoyance into a documented one.

**Evidence:** How it works here: an opt-out is caught by a fixed rule rather than left to the model to interpret, it is written to the resident's record permanently, and cohort selection consults it on every future run rather than at send time only. The related discipline that the opt-out reply still routes to a person, because someone asking you to stop emailing them is usually telling you something else as well.

**Takeaway:** Test your own opt-out by opting a test address out, then running your next campaign and checking whether it was consulted. Most people have never run that test.

**Discipline:** Keep it short and unglamorous. This is a small correct thing done properly, not a headline feature, and inflating it would be out of register.

## 11. The last cohort gets no email at all

**Format:** text, 250-300 words · **Pillar:** what the automation is allowed to do · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** Open on the group you would most expect an automation to chase, and the fact that it is the one group the system will not message.

**The turn:** At thirty days out the resident is inside the renewal-offer window. Anything arriving from the property at that point is read as part of the offer, whatever it actually says, and a soft "is everything okay" landing next to a rate increase reads as a tactic. So the thirty-day cohort produces an internal handoff to whoever runs leasing, and nothing goes to the resident. The automation's job at that point is to shut up and tell a person.

**Evidence:** The four windows and what each does: 120 is a watchlist with no contact at all, 90 is the check-in, 60 is a nudge only to residents who never replied, 30 is an internal handoff with no resident email. The reasoning for the 60-day rule specifically, which is that nudging someone who already answered is how a check-in becomes a nuisance.

**Takeaway:** Every outreach cadence needs a defined point where it stops. If yours does not have one, it will eventually contact someone at the exact moment contact does damage.

**Discipline:** Do not present restraint as a feature to be admired. State it as a rule with a reason, in the same register as any other rule.

## 12. My own test email came back as a resident reply

**Format:** text, 250-300 words · **Pillar:** failure lessons · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** Open on the verification step that broke the thing it was verifying.

**The turn:** Checking that the endpoints were alive meant calling them, and calling them ran them. The reply check went to the mailbox, found a message from a rehearsal an hour earlier, and processed it as though it had just arrived. Nothing was sent and no resident existed, but the state was wrong — and the same defect during a recorded demo would have produced an alert about a conversation that happened yesterday, live, with no way to explain it. The bug was not in the logic. It was in the assumption that anything in the inbox belonged to this run.

**Evidence:** The specific defect and the specific fix: the reply check now ignores any message that arrived before the demo's first send, and because a reset clears every send time, leftover mail from an earlier rehearsal is structurally invisible rather than merely filtered. The verification that it holds — a stale reply left sitting in the mailbox, automatic polling running throughout, and no stray events recorded.

**Takeaway:** If your automation reads a shared inbox, ask what stops it acting on something that was already there. "It only looks at new messages" is a claim about the sender's behaviour, not about your system.

**Discipline:** Own that this was found by accident during routine verification, not by design. The honest version is more useful.

## 14. What this cannot tell you

**Format:** text, 250-300 words · **Pillar:** what the automation is allowed to do · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** Open with the claim not being made, before anyone has to ask for it.

**The turn:** It does not predict who will renew, it does not price anything, and it has never improved a renewal rate, because no client has run it. What it does is narrower and checkable: it asks a question at a fixed point, routes what comes back, and records what was found and whether it was closed. Whether that changes renewals needs a full cycle, a baseline and a comparison group, and anyone claiming the outcome before running that has skipped the part that matters.

**Evidence:** The verification that does exist, stated as testing rather than results: forty synthetic residents, fourteen of fourteen drafts passing the checks, a refused duplicate send, a real reply matched by reference code and classified correctly, an issue closed and confirmed. The live variable a pilot turns on, which is reply rate — a check-in nobody answers produces nothing, and that number should be measured early rather than assumed.

**Takeaway:** Ask anyone selling retention automation what their claimed improvement was measured against, and over what period. If the answer is a case study with no baseline, it is a story.

**Discipline:** Build 1 posts 13 and 14 own the buyer's-checklist framing. **This is self-disclosure, not a checklist.** Stay in first person about this build's own limits. The closing question is one line, not a list. Never present fixture verification as a customer result.

## 15. Send me your next quarter of expirations
