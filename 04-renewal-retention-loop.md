## ⚠️ Honesty Note Up Front

Of the four products, this has **the weakest evidence base**, and you should know exactly where it's thin before you sell it.

**What's solidly evidenced:** turnover is expensive, retention matters, and the 90-day outreach window is best practice across many independent sources.

**What's thinly evidenced:** the claim that renewal outreach is an *unsolved* gap. That assertion traces mainly to companies selling renewal automation. No neutral industry survey examined named renewal outreach as a pain point property managers raise unprompted.

**What this means practically:** don't lead with this. Sell it as an expansion into an account that already trusts you, where the client has already told you retention matters to them. Leading with a partially-evidenced problem to a cold prospect is how you burn a first meeting.

## The Problem

### What actually happens

Nobody asks the tenant how things are going until it's time to ask them to re-sign.

The formal renewal offer goes out at 45–60 days, often carrying a rent increase, to a tenant the manager hasn't had a non-transactional conversation with in eleven months. If that tenant is sitting on an unresolved grievance — a repair that took three weeks, a neighbour complaint that went nowhere, a deposit question never answered — the manager doesn't know.

By the time they find out the tenant isn't renewing, the tenant has already toured other places.

### The core insight

> *"The conventional wisdom of a 30-day notice window is a legal minimum, not a retention strategy."*[^1]

The decision to stay or leave is made weeks before any formal notice arrives — shaped by how well the manager communicated, how quickly maintenance was resolved, and whether the resident feels valued or feels like a line on a spreadsheet.[^2]

### The mechanism that connects this to money

This is the part worth understanding, because it's what makes the product coherent rather than just "send more emails":

**A resident with an open, unresolved maintenance request is a retention risk. Clearing that issue before presenting a renewal offer removes a significant friction point.**[^1]

That's the whole product in one sentence. You're not automating renewal reminders — you're running a scheduled grievance-detection sweep 90 days out, while there's still time to fix what you find.

### Why it stays broken

Because the renewal process is treated as administrative. Someone runs the report, sends the offer, chases the signature. The report tells you *which leases expire*; it tells you nothing about *which tenants are wavering and why*.

Most property managers send one renewal notice and wait. The most effective programs send multiple touchpoints across the 90-day window, mixing automated messages with personal outreach.[^1]

### What the software they already own does — and doesn't

**Buildium does** identify all leases expiring in 120 days or less and present them in a dedicated renewals portal.[^3] It handles renewal document generation and e-signature execution competently.

**Buildium does not** run proactive tenant engagement in advance of that — no structured check-in, no grievance detection, no sentiment capture, no routing of at-risk residents to a human.

**Be careful here.** This is a "your software does this shallowly" argument, which is materially weaker than "your software doesn't do this at all." Don't overstate it.

## Real-Life Examples

### Example 1 — The invisible grievance

> A repair took three weeks in February. It got fixed. The tenant never complained again — she just quietly stopped feeling good about the place.
>
> In October, 45 days from lease end, the renewal offer arrives with a $75 increase. She's already toured two places. She declines.
>
> The manager never knew there was anything to fix. Turnover cost: roughly $4,000 in vacancy, make-ready, marketing, and re-leasing — against a grievance that a phone call in March would have resolved.

### Example 2 — The recoverable one

> Same portfolio, different unit. At 90 days, the tenant gets a short check-in: *"Hi Maria — you're coming up on two years with us in March. Before we talk about the next term, is there anything that hasn't been sorted out?"*
>
> She replies: the bathroom extractor fan has been noisy since summer and she reported it twice.
>
> It's fixed in four days. At 60 days, the renewal offer goes out. She signs.
>
> The fan cost $140. The alternative cost $4,000.

**Example 2 is the sale.** It's concrete, small, and the arithmetic is undeniable.

### Example 3 — The one that reframes the product

> A manager runs the 90-day sweep across 14 expiring leases and gets four responses flagging open issues. Three are fixable. One tenant says they're relocating for work regardless.
>
> That fourth response is also valuable — the unit gets listed six weeks earlier than it otherwise would have been, cutting the vacancy gap to almost nothing.

Use Example 3 with sceptical buyers. It shows the product wins even when retention fails.

## Evidence This Is a Real Problem

### What's well evidenced

**Turnover cost.** $3,872–$4,000 per unit appears across multiple independent sources. NAA benchmarks show turnover costs up 17.5% year over year, with vacancy and rent loss rising for four consecutive years.[^4]

**Maintenance drives non-renewal.** Slow maintenance response is identified as the #1 factor in tenant non-renewal decisions.[^5] Independently, **31% of tenants uncertain about renewing said they would stay if their manager were more responsive to maintenance**,[^6] and **40% of tenants would renew if the manager invested more in property maintenance.**[^7]

That's the evidential link between "ask about grievances" and "retain the tenant."

**The timing window is consistently documented.** Independent sources converge on the same structure:[^1][^2][^8][^9]

| Days out | Action |
|---|---|
| **120–90** | Review expiring leases; begin planning |
| **90** | Soft, relationship-oriented check-in — *not* a formal notice |
| **60** | Formal renewal offer |
| **30** | Personal follow-up if no response |

**Retention is a growth lever.** Referrals from existing clients are the #1 growth channel for property management companies, and renewal rate improvements from roughly 55% to 72% are attributed to resident experience across the preceding lease term rather than to the renewal process itself.[^10]

### What's thinly evidenced

**No neutral source names renewal outreach as a top pain point.** Unlike maintenance (which appears in manager surveys, owner surveys, non-renewal data, and outside corroboration) and make-ready (which appears in NAA cost benchmarks), this did not surface as a named, unprompted problem in any neutral industry source examined.

**The gap claim comes from interested parties.** The specific assertion that PMS renewal features are "a notification, not a workflow" originates from companies selling the automation layer that fills that gap.

**Grade this honestly:** the *retention problem* is Tier 1 evidenced. The *outreach workflow gap* is Tier 4.

### Profile

| Attribute | Target |
|---|---|
| Doors | 200–600 |
| Relationship | **Existing client, ideally** — this is an expansion sale |
| Lease structure | Annual leases with staggered end dates |

### Strong buying signals

- They can quote their renewal rate — means they track it and it bothers them
- They've recently lost tenants they expected to keep
- They've told you turnover is their biggest expense
- They already buy from you and trust the relationship
- Their owners are asking about tenant retention

### Qualifying questions

1. *"What's your renewal rate?"* — if they don't know, that's a different conversation
2. *"When do you first contact a tenant about renewing?"* — listen for 30 or 45 days
3. *"How do you find out a tenant is unhappy before they give notice?"* — usually a pause
4. *"How do you get lease end dates out of your system today?"* — establishes the data path

### Disqualifiers

- Month-to-month heavy portfolios — no renewal cycle to work with
- Very high natural turnover markets (student housing) where retention isn't the strategy
- **A cold prospect.** Genuinely — this is not a first-sale product

## Positioning and Sales

### The critical reframe

**Do not sell this as renewal automation. Sell it as grievance detection.**

| ❌ Weak framing | ✅ Strong framing |
|---|---|
| "Automates your renewal reminders" | "Finds out who's unhappy while you can still fix it" |
| "Never miss a renewal deadline" | "Catches the broken extractor fan before it costs you $4,000" |
| "Streamlines renewal outreach" | "Turns your lease expiry report into a retention pipeline" |

The first column collides directly with Buildium's renewals portal and loses. The second describes something their software genuinely doesn't do.

### The framing sentence

> *"Your system tells you which leases expire. It doesn't tell you which tenants are already halfway out the door. This asks them, 90 days out, while there's still time to do something about it."*

### The arithmetic close

> *"You've got 14 leases expiring in the next quarter. If this saves one tenant who would otherwise have left, that's about $4,000. I'm charging you $300 a month."*

One retained tenant per year pays for the product several times over. That's an easy number for an owner to accept.

### Objection handling

**"Buildium already handles renewals."**
> "It does — it shows you what's expiring and handles the paperwork and e-signature. That's the administrative half. This is the ninety days before that, where you find out the reason someone's going to say no. Different job."

**"Won't tenants find this annoying?"**
> "It's one short message asking if anything needs sorting, once a year, ninety days out. Most tenants read that as attentive rather than intrusive — and the ones who reply are telling you something worth knowing. You approve every message before it goes."

**"We already ask people how things are going."**
> "When, and to how many? The value isn't the question — it's that it goes to all fourteen, on schedule, and the answers land somewhere you'll actually see them."

**"What if someone replies with something serious?"**
> "It routes straight to you with the full context. It never tries to resolve anything itself — its only job is to surface it early."

## The Build

### What the automation does

1. **Reads** lease end dates from a CSV or the renewal report their PMS already exports — **no PDF parsing, no API**
2. **Buckets** tenants into 120 / 90 / 60 / 30-day cohorts
3. **Generates** a personalised check-in at 90 days, referencing tenure and specifics
4. **Sends** by email or SMS after human approval
5. **Captures** replies and classifies intent: STAYING / WAVERING / LEAVING / GRIEVANCE
6. **Extracts** the specific issue when a grievance is mentioned
7. **Routes** anything other than a clean "staying" to a human, with full context
8. **Escalates** non-responders into the 60-day formal offer stage
9. **Displays** a renewal pipeline board by month, with at-risk tenants flagged

### The message design is the whole product

At 90 days this must be a **soft, relationship-oriented touchpoint — not a formal notice.**[^1] Get this wrong and it reads as a rent-increase warning, which produces exactly the outcome you're trying to prevent.

**Personalisation signals that matter:**[^2]
- Length of residency ("you're coming up on two years")
- Upcoming lease milestone
- Acknowledgement of long-term tenancy value
- **Never** address it to "Resident"

**What the 90-day message must NOT contain:**
- Rent figures
- Renewal terms
- Deadlines
- Anything that looks like a legal notice

Its only job is to open a door.

### Intent classification schema

```json
{
  "tenant_id": "string",
  "intent": "STAYING | WAVERING | LEAVING | GRIEVANCE | UNCLEAR",
  "grievance_present": "boolean",
  "grievance_summary": "string | null",
  "grievance_category": "MAINTENANCE | NEIGHBOURS | FEES | COMMUNICATION | PROPERTY_CONDITION | OTHER | null",
  "actionable": "boolean",
  "urgency": "HIGH | MEDIUM | LOW",
  "suggested_human_action": "string",
  "confidence": "HIGH | MEDIUM | LOW"
}
```

**The rule: anything that is not a clean, unambiguous STAYING goes to a human.** The system's job is detection and routing, never resolution.

### n8n architecture

```
[Schedule Trigger: daily 08:00]
        ↓
[Google Sheets / CSV: read lease roster]
        ↓
[Code node: compute days-to-expiry, assign cohort]
        ↓
[Switch: 90d / 60d / 30d / none]
        ↓
[Filter: already contacted this cohort? → skip]
        ↓
[AI node: personalise message]     ← Haiku-class
        ↓
[Approval queue: manager reviews batch]
        ↓
[Gmail / SMS: send]
        ↓
[Sheets: log outreach + set awaiting-reply flag]

──── reply handling ────

[Email Trigger (IMAP) / SMS webhook]
        ↓
[Code node: match reply → tenant record]
        ↓
[AI node: classify intent → JSON]
        ↓
[Switch: STAYING → log | everything else → alert]
        ↓
[Telegram: notify manager with full context]
        ↓
[Sheets: update pipeline status]
```

**Node notes:**
- **No Buildium integration.** The client exports their renewal report to CSV, or you read a shared Google Sheet they maintain. This sidesteps the $400/month API tier entirely[^11]
- **Batch approval matters.** Don't ask the manager to approve 14 messages individually — present them as one reviewable list with inline editing
- Use **Email Trigger (IMAP)** for replies;[^12] SMS webhook if they're on SMS
- Rate-limit sends. Fourteen identical-looking emails at 08:00 from one address looks like spam

|---|---|

| Message generation + approval queue | 0.5 day |

Simplest build of the four, and the most robust — non-response doesn't break anything, it just means no reply to classify.

## The Demo

Moderate impact — this is the least visually dramatic of the four. Structure it to compensate.

**Pre-load** a fake roster of 40 leases with staggered end dates.

**On the call:**

1. **Show the pipeline board first.** "Here's every lease expiring in the next four months, grouped by window." Simple, immediately legible.
2. Highlight the 90-day cohort — 14 tenants.
3. **Trigger generation.** Fourteen personalised messages appear, each referencing tenure and specifics. Open two side by side to show they're genuinely different, not mail-merged.
4. **Show the approval queue.** "Nothing sends until you've read them."
5. **Run a live reply.** Paste in: *"We're happy here but the extractor fan in the bathroom has been broken since summer and I've reported it twice."*
6. Watch it classify: GRIEVANCE / MAINTENANCE / actionable / HIGH — and alert the manager with the issue extracted.
7. **Close on that specific example:** "That fan costs $140 to fix. That tenant leaving costs you $4,000."

**The close:**

> *"Export your next quarter's expiring leases to a CSV and send it over. I'll generate the ninety-day messages so you can see exactly what your tenants would receive."*

## Risks and Limitations

| Risk | Reality | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Weakest evidence of the four** | Gap claim is competitor-asserted | Don't lead with it. Sell into existing accounts |
| **Slowest to prove** | Needs a full renewal cycle — months | Report leading indicators: response rate, grievances surfaced, issues resolved |
| **Partially covered by their PMS** | Buildium has a renewals portal[^3] | Position as pre-renewal engagement, not renewal administration |
| **Messages go out in client's name** | Higher trust ask than COI or triage | Batch approval before every send, always |
| **Notice period rules vary** | 30–90 days depending on jurisdiction[^8] | Make windows configurable. Never generate legal notices |
| **Tone risk** | A cold 90-day message reads as a rent warning | No rent figures, no terms, no deadlines at 90 days |

### The rule to never break

**Never let this system generate or send a legal notice.** No notices to vacate, no notices of rent increase, no lease non-renewal notices. Those are jurisdiction-specific legal documents with statutory content and delivery requirements. Your product opens a conversation; the client's existing process handles the paperwork.
